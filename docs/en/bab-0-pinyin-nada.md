# Chapter 0 · 0.1–0.5: Pinyin & Tones

## 0.1: What is Mandarin, anyway?

What we're learning is called **普通话 (pǔtōnghuà)**, literally "the common language". This is standard Mandarin used in mainland China, taught in schools, and used in the HSK exam.


| Hanzi | Pinyin | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 普通话 | pǔtōnghuà | Standard Mandarin |
| 汉字 | hànzì | Mandarin characters |
| 拼音 | pīnyīn | system for writing Mandarin sounds with Latin letters |


## 0.2: Simplified vs Traditional: why do we use simplified?

Hanzi comes in two versions: **simplified** (fewer strokes, used in mainland China & Singapore) and **traditional** (the classical forms, used in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macau). The simplified version was designed to be easier to learn.

This book, and the HSK exam, use **simplified**. Here are some examples of the difference:

| Simplified | Traditional | Pinyin | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 汉语 | 漢語 | Hànyǔ | Mandarin language |
| 钱 | 錢 | qián | money |
| 爱 | 愛 | ài | love |
| 你好 | 你好 | nǐ hǎo | hello (same in both versions) |

::: info
You'll sometimes run into traditional in the real world, like Threads captions from Taiwan. Don't panic: many look similar, and you can guess the patterns once you understand hanzi components (covered in [Chapter 3](/en/bab-3-radikal-bedah-hanzi)).
:::

## 0.3: Pinyin: writing sounds with Latin letters

One Mandarin syllable = **initial** (the opening sound) + **final** (the vowel sound) + **tone**. Example: `hǎo` = `h` + `ao` + tone 3.

<div class="kenapa">
<div class="kenapa-title">🧩 Why pinyin use Latin letters instead of its own symbols?</div>
<p>Pinyin was designed in the 1950s (Zhou Youguang's team) to fight illiteracy, Latin letters were chosen because they were already the international standard: easy to print, easy to type, and familiar to foreigners. Before that there was Wade-Giles (writing "t'a" for tā, fiddly with apostrophes) and Zhuyin/Bopomofo (ㄅㄆㄇㄈ, unique symbols still used in Taiwan today).</p>
<p><strong>Same pattern:</strong> Taiwan uses Zhuyin (ㄅㄆㄇㄈ) for exactly the same sounds, different "packaging", same content.</p>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> only the SHAPE of the letters was borrowed, not their sounds, q, x, c, zh are not pronounced like English at all. Don't trust your English instincts here.</p>
</div>

<div class="kenapa">
<div class="kenapa-title">🧩 Why are y/w in the initial table?</div>
<p>y and w there are actually <strong>not consonants</strong>, they're "separators". Syllables starting with i/u/ü would be ambiguous written plain ("ian", one syllable or i+an?), so pinyin inserts y/w to make syllable boundaries clear: i → yi (一 yī), u → wu (五 wǔ), ü → yu (月 yuè). They add ZERO sound, yī sounds exactly like "ī" would if "ī" were allowed.</p>
<p><strong>Same pattern:</strong> 一 yī, 有 yǒu, 五 wǔ, 月 yuè, compare with 学 xué (has a real initial x, doesn't need y/w).</p>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> don't pronounce an English "y" consonant in yī, there's no "y" sound there, it's just spelling scaffolding.</p>
</div>

The ones that don't sound like you'd expect:

- **j** ≈ "j" (like "jeep" but softer), **q** ≈ "ch" (like "cheek", with a hiss), **x** ≈ "sh" (like "sheep")
- **ü**, round your lips like you're about to say "oo", but say "ee" instead
- **zh / ch / sh / r**, curl your tongue back; they sound heavier than plain z / c / s

| Pattern | Rule | HSK 1 examples |
|---|---|---|

<div class="bedah"><strong>去</strong> (qù, to go), originally qǜ, but since ü meets q, the two dots are dropped in writing. It's still read "qü", not "qu".</div>

<div class="kenapa">
<div class="kenapa-title">🧩 Why ü lose its two dots after j/q/x?</div>
<p>Because j, q, x <strong>never</strong> meet a plain u, they can only meet ü. So "qu" can't be ambiguous: there's no "koo" sound after q to distinguish from. The dots are dropped to make typing easy. Same logic for y: yu, yue, yun are always ü. But n and l <strong>can</strong> meet both u and ü, so the dots must stay to avoid mix-ups.</p>
<p><strong>Same pattern:</strong> 去 qù (= qǜ), 学 xué (= xüé), 月 yuè (= üè), dots dropped; vs 女 nǚ, dots kept.</p>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> the spelling lies, the sound doesn't, "qu" is still read "qü" (lips rounded like ü), not "koo".</p>
</div>

<div class="kenapa">
<div class="kenapa-title">🧩 Why tone marks look like that?</div>
<p>The four tone marks are actually <strong>melody drawings</strong>: ˉ flat-high, ˊ rising like asking "huh?", ˇ dips then rises, ˋ falls sharply. The marks literally draw your voice's movement, you can almost "read" the tone just from the shape. That's why the neutral tone has no mark: it's read short and light, with no fixed melody to draw.</p>
<p><strong>Same pattern:</strong> 妈 mā (flat), 麻 má (rising), 马 mǎ (dipping), 骂 mà (falling), one "ma" sound, four drawings, four melodies.</p>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> in fast speech, tone 3 rarely does the full dip-and-rise, usually just a low fall. The ˇ mark is the "dictionary" version; the casual version is only half of it.</p>
</div>

The neutral tone (often called "tone 5") is written **with no tone mark**: short and light. Examples: 妈妈 **māma** (mother), 谢谢 **xièxie** (thank you).

<div class="kenapa">
<div class="kenapa-title">🧩 Why 吗/呢/的 neutral in tone?</div>
<p>Grammar particles are the "supporting actors" of a sentence, they carry no meaning of their own, only function (吗 = question marker, 的 = possessive linker, 呢 = follow-up question marker). Since they're unimportant meaning-wise, they're said quickly and lightly, with no emphasis. No tone mark = no fixed melody = "just skim it". The missing mark IS the information.</p>
<p><strong>Same pattern:</strong> 吗 ma, 呢 ne, 的 de, 了 le, all neutral, all grammar particles.</p>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> when you see a syllable with no tone mark, don't read it with full emphasis, read it short and relaxed. That's the difference between 妈 mā (tone 1, "mother", important) vs 吗 ma (neutral, "just a question marker").</p>
</div>

**Tone mark placement rule:** if there's an **a**, put it on the a. No a? Put it on **o** or **e**. Only **i/u** left? Put it on the **last** letter: l**iù**, h**uì**, j**iǔ**.

<div class="kenapa">
<div class="kenapa-title">🧩 Why tone mark go on 'a' first?</div>
<p>The rule is a &gt; o &gt; e &gt; i/u, and it's not arbitrary. The tone mark goes on the most "open" vowel (the loudest to voice): <strong>a</strong> is the most open, then <strong>o</strong>, then <strong>e</strong>. That's why hǎo's mark is on a (not o), and hē's mark is on e (since there's no a/o). With only i/u left, the mark goes on the <strong>last</strong> letter: liù, huì, jiǔ.</p>
<p><strong>Same pattern:</strong> mā (only has a), hǎo (a beats o), hē (only has e), jiǔ (i/u → the last one).</p>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> ü always carries its own mark (nǚ, lǜ), and ui/iu are actually abbreviations of uei/iou, which is why the mark lands on the hidden "main vowel" (huì = h+uei, jiǔ = j+iou).</p>
</div>

::: tip
**Bonus: tone-3 sandhi.** Two tone 3s meet → the first becomes tone 2. That's why 你好 (nǐ + hǎo) is read **ní hǎo**, 我想 (wǒ + xiǎng) is read **wó xiǎng**, 买水 (mǎi + shuǐ) is read **mái shuǐ**. In this book pinyin is always written in base tones (nǐ hǎo), but your mouth adjusts when speaking.
:::

<div class="kenapa">
<div class="kenapa-title">🧩 Why tones keep changing (sandhi)?</div>
<p>Your mouth is lazy, and sandhi is how your mouth saves energy. Two "heavy" tones in a row get softened to be easier to say: 不 (a sharply falling tone 4) before another tone 4 feels like barking twice, so it softens into tone 2 (bú shì). Two tone 3s in a row (both dipping) tire the tongue, so the first rises into tone 2 (ní hǎo). Same as in English: "going to" becomes "gonna" in fast speech.</p>
<p><strong>Same pattern:</strong> 你好 ní hǎo, 我想 wó xiǎng, 买水 mái shuǐ, 不去 bú qù, 一个 yí gè, all your mouth looking for shortcuts.</p>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> only your MOUTH changes, not the writing, that's why this book still writes nǐ hǎo (base tones). Sandhi is a speaking rule, not a writing rule.</p>
</div>
